After reading Iris Meredith's [There's no such thing as Just a
Tool](https://deadsimpletech.com/blog/no-such-thing-as-just-a-tool), I was
reminded of some related work.

If you've been thinking along Torvalds' line (it's "just a tool"), you might be
interested in reflecting upon the above article. Meredith gives a fascinating
analysis of how human–tool combinations are not value-neutral despite how we
might idealize them. I see clear dialogue here with questions like "Can you
separate the creator from their work?" and "How do we embody values in our
technology? Who gets to choose what values are embodied?"

Related work along those themes:

- [Greg Wilson's "Rainy Day Thoughts on
  AI"](https://third-bit.com/2026/08/04/rainy-day-thoughts-on-ai/) (first
  mentioned in [Digital Etiquette]({% link _posts/2026-08-15-signal-boost.md
  %})) opens with Orwell & Dali (creator vs. work)
- Ruha Benjamin's excellent book _Race After Technology_ provokes, prods, and
  pokes many of these questions with recent examples, including software built
  on statistical models (from, _e.g._, predictive policing and other legal
  system applications to things as supposedly innocuous as Google Maps).
- I naïvely engaged this question (or maybe sidestepped it) when [thinking about
  LLMs in January]({% link _posts/2026-01-29-llms-chat.md %}): I took the "tool
  conversation" (how to use an LLM effectively) for granted and wished to set it
  aside for a different but related conversation about power and harm. Thanks to
  this post's impetus, I'm now reconsidering that the 2 conversations fit in the
  same framework---using an LLM engenders different priorities and goals, which
  are evidently related to power and harm. We exercise power to achieve our
  goals, and we might want to decide how to do so while minimizing harm.

I also stumbled across a few related articles (least useful to most):

- Bryan Tan wrote [AI is not "just a
  tool"](https://lucidthemes.substack.com/p/ai-is-not-just-a-tool-53557d39bc5e).
  He asks a few ok questions:

  > What does the tool do? What is the scope of its operations? What will it
  > replace? How will it effect our lifestyles?

  But, in my view, he performs a kind of
  [criti-hype](https://peoples-things.ghost.io/youre-doing-it-wrong-notes-on-criticism-and-technology-hype/),
  which Cory Doctorow has [introduced me to more than
  once](https://pluralistic.net/tag/criti-hype/). Tan writes

  > moralizing about technology can be beside the point. It isn’t necessary in
  > this discussion to talk about tech’s inherent goodness or badness. We can
  > limit ourselves to pondering its effects. We just have to turn our eyes
  > away from how these tools will enable us to shape the world, and start
  > looking at how using these tools will shape us.

  and then further, disguised in what appears on the surface to be critical,
  promotes the technology:

  > We’re in the nascent days of this technology, where the products that are
  > going to shape the future are just now hitting the public at scale in
  > their most primitive forms. We’re at a critical juncture where we need to
  > think deeply about what some of these effects might be.
  >
  > […]
  >
  > [AI's] arrival brings not one new medium to reckon with, but countless,
  > and the obsolescence of just as many.

  So rather than engage with the good questions (such as how we are shaped by
  the human--tool gestalt), he asserts that "AI" will obsolete many media and
  create new ones, that it will shape the future. I suppose that's vague
  enough to be hard to falsify, and thus probably true. Yet it can't help but
  leave the "tool" question short.

- [Christine Haskell](https://christinehaskell.com/) wrote [AI Is Not Just A
  Tool](https://christinehaskell.substack.com/p/ai-is-not-just-a-tool), which I
  think wants to make a deep point: most tools engage us with our
  reality, while "AI" proposes (or invents!) a different reality. That's a
  really interesting thesis, from which we ask more questions: who determines
  the shapes of those realities? Whose values are thus embodied? What might
  reality distortions through the lens of these tools do to our psyches?

  Haskell goes on to write about legal and regulatory differences between a tool
  and a system, which naturally calls into question responsibility. Are these
  things inherently [accountability
  sinks](https://aworkinglibrary.com/writing/accountability-sinks), or are there
  other modes? What do we do when things go wrong? Better to understand that
  "systems acting on the public [should be] answerable to the public." Yet today
  it so often seems our public systems place vastly more burden on harmed
  individuals, and make it harder for them to claim and redress harm, than they
  do to regulate and hold accountable said systems. (Fortunately, [this is a
  matter of policy, and policy can
  change](https://www.cbc.ca/radio/podcastnews/who-broke-the-internet-understood-transcripts-listen-1.7611600).)

  But here's why I still find this engagement with the tool question not as
  helpful as others: from the second paragraph onward the writing reeks of LLM
  output. I nearly didn't finish the article, and many sentences become
  meaningless for their flowery. I find this disappointing from such an
  intellectually distinguished individual who prides herself on "keeping values
  in the room." It's not that the LLM _can't_ be helpful in writing; it's that
  the generated text so clearly distracts from or obscures her useful points. I
  have a hard time discerning which values are in the room with me.

- Finally, [HCI and data visualization and accessibility researcher Frank
  Elavsky](https://www.frank.computer/) wrote [Stop saying that AI is just a
  tool and it only matters how it is
  used](https://www.frank.computer/blog/2025/05/just-a-tool.html). Watch the
  whole talk, too.

  Unsurprisingly, this article takes as given that ["artifacts have
  politics"](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~beki/cs4001/Winner.pdf) and
  interrogates how "just a tool" is used. In Elavsky's conception, it is
  "deceptively misleading," "deceptively simple," and "insultingly naive": in
  other words, might it only serve to shut down or limit the conversation, just
  as Meredith writes? Uttering this phrase doesn't contribute anything
  meaningful to our current discourse.

  Elavsky is also concerned by the usual follow up, "it matters how you use it."
  Besides refusing to engage with centuries of thinking about morality ([as do
  the answers at time of writing to the StackExchange question "Ethics of using
  AI in creative work"](https://philosophy.stackexchange.com/q/141228)---no
  wonder Felienne Hermans is so concerned with [CS not engaging with other
  fields](https://www.felienne.nl/csoc-s01e03/)!), this phrase also participates
  in a tradition of individualism that some credit with destroying community and
  blinding us to structural, systematic change that might solve some of our most
  pressing problems.

  I am thus utterly unsurprised that Elavsky also cites Heidegger as Meredith
  did.

  I won't summarize all (the rest) of Elavsky's engagement with this question
  (sneak peaks: getting rid of all pain and flattening our being are not good
  results; find the street's use for these things; "go and do what machines
  cannot"). Really, you should go read. Engage with the question yourself: what
  tools shape you, and how?
