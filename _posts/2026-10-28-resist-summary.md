A thought from [I Don’t Want to Read What You Didn’t
Write](https://blog.colinbreck.com/i-dont-want-to-read-what-you-didnt-write/).

Colin Breck wrote his article in the spirit of [many others]({% link
pages/etiquette.md %}) on the LLMish being shoved into our eyeballs. At the end,
he quotes Simon Sarris's [Resist
Summary](https://map.simonsarris.com/p/resist-summary):

> If you ask a machine to do something for you, you are at risk of not asking
> for what you want, but what it can do well. If what it can do well is
> summarize, and you rely upon the tool, you may tune your questions so that you
> get better answers. This looks like training a machine to do what you want,
> but its also training yourself to ask questions about the world in a certain
> way. What are the questions you are omitting?

I've [written before]({% link _posts/2026-09-20-turkle.md %}) about the effects
of data and machine-oriented thinking on our conversations. Even if you doubt
that [LLMs affect our language]({% link _posts/2026-05-07-machines-humans.md
%}), it is clear that [using tools affects us]({% link
_posts/2026-09-02-tool-dialogue.md %}), and so I think that _of course_ using a
machine to answer a question will twist the questions we ask.

That's but a twist on [Goodhart's
Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law): "When a measure becomes a
target, it ceases to be a good measure." Or, how we arrive at answers will
change the questions we ask. Everything we do changes incentives, and we must be
careful lest we incentivize the wrong behavior.

Perniciously, as Simon points out, it can *feel* like we are bending the machine
even as it also bends us. We know from [Configuration Work research]({% link
_posts/2026-10-02-reading-notes-configuration-work.md %}) that this is a normal
outcome of integrating LLMs in the workplace.

Remember, the machines have only ever existed to facilitate our human goals!
