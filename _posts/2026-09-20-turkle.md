*The Second Self* in conversation with [Just a Tool]({% link
_posts/2026-09-02-tool-dialogue.md %}).

Thanks again to Felienne Hermans and Hanna Schraffenberger via [Computer
Science, Off Course! Episode 2](https://www.felienne.nl/csoc-s01e07/), which
covered this topic. I listened to the episode on the heels of writing my
previous post, and wow! What a connection there is.

*The Second Self* is [Sherry Turkle's 1984
book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherry_Turkle#The_Second_Self) about our
relationship with computers. There's so much to unpack, but I want to in
particular call attention that even back then folks questioned the (now
Torvaldian?) notion that computers and their programs are "just a tool."

Hermans says the key concept of Turkle's book is what a computer does to you,
what working with a computer happens to you. In the introduction, Turkle makes
her position clear: "I'm writing against the common view that a computer is
just a tool."

Like we saw previously, the use of any tool creates a combined "gestalt". Both
parts influence each other, leaving each marked in some way. Turkle's analysis
of programmers and programming education of her time shows us that one way
computer mark programmers is by giving them little fiefdoms. It is exciting and
awe-inspiring to have a corner of the universe that is utterly, despotically
mine, which I can shape to my will! For many, it's a pleasant escape from a
world that otherwise tries to destroy them. Perhaps, though, it is no surprise
that this particular phenomenon of absolute power gives rise to real-world
tyrants who insist on imposing their will on the rest of us. We need only look
at [who controls the language of LLMs]({% link _posts/2026-05-07-machines-humans.md %})
or [the supposed intellectual superiors of Silicon Valley](https://www.iankduncan.com/personal/2026-09-16-sex-ai-and-the-apocalypse/)
to see the extrapolation from "control over my personal universe" to "control
over all people's universe."

Of course, computers also provide the means of production, privacy, and
coordination to dissenters and activists working against authoritarian control.
Is this all the same reflection of mastery over one's own dominion, with the
former the [mirror-world warping](https://pluralistic.net/2023/09/05/not-that-naomi/#if-the-naomi-be-klein-youre-doing-just-fine)
of the latter?

Moreover, as Hermans explains Turkle's telling, the original AI program
computer-washed our knowledge of the world. The "computerized knowledge" becomes
superior (perhaps seen as [more objective](https://www.felienne.nl/csoc-s01e09/)?)
by means of being computerized, a tautology if ever there was one. We've
seen this in the decades since: we readily acknowledge the cultural memes of
technology affecting knowledge and truth.

- "it must be true, it was on the internet!"
- "someone was wrong on the internet again"

So we should not be surprised that the LLM project is another (channeling
Turkle) colonial project that controls and shapes knowledge, how we produce it,
and which knowledge we value.

As Hermans quotes:

> […] since you cannot really master all the disciplines that you have designs
> on, you need confidence your knowledge makes the traditional wisdom of these
> fields unworthy of serious consideration.

Hah! Today's LLM builders have the hubris to think that the textual average of
human output (plus or minus their control what goes in and what comes out?)
somehow represents _better_ than the experts of real fields.

## [Reclaiming Conversation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherry_Turkle#Reclaiming_Conversation)

The 2015 title from Turkle looks again at what computers do to us. Hermans cites
Turkle's example of mourning:

> Does tracking mourning help us to mourn, or does it deflect us because we feel
> we must start and end our story with a number? **Does it limit the stories
> that we tell?**

(emphasis mine)

I'll share a personal example that rhymes with Hermans on the podcast: when I
ask my wife how she slept, she sometimes tells me her Ōura score from memory or
after checking on her phone. I often respond (perhaps with exasperation…): but
how do *you* feel about how you slept? Do you agree with the score or not?

Unfortunately, I fear that Turkle's question is hard to answer from the inside.
Thanks to much research, we know that we are particularly bad at evaluating the
impact of LLMs on ourselves while we use them. Perhaps that is true of all
computers. I can't think of anyone I am close to who would claim that their use
of (say) ChatGPT limits the stories they tell. And yet… mightn't it? How can we
even know, since we don't see the alternate universe in which they told other
stories? At best, we can perhaps try to compare our conversational ability on
the LLM pill and detoxed from it? I already know that *my* ability to converse
with my wife over dinner is better when I have engaged my brain then when I
passively consume.

So what might using computers do to me? What might using them do to you?
